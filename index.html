What is a pod?

A home for your data, on the web, that you own.

The web was built so anyone could publish. Then data moved into a few giant databases, and every app kept its own copy of you. Solid, started by Sir Tim Berners-Lee (who invented the web), flips that around. You get a pod: a personal data store with a web address. Apps stop hoarding your data. They come to your pod, ask for exactly what they need, and you can say no, or say yes until Friday.

❌ Today: you are scattered

  • Six copies of you, six breach risks, six privacy policies.
  • Consent is a checkbox you clicked once, forever.
  • Leave an app and your history stays behind.

✅ With a pod: one home, many guests

  • One copy, in a place you control. Apps get access, not ownership.
  • Every permission is visible, has an end date, and can be taken back.
  • Switch apps and your data does not move. The new app just asks.
🏠

A pod is storage

A small web server for your stuff: files and structured data (your allergies, your receipts, your contacts) in open formats any app can read.

🪪

A WebID is you

One web address that is your identity. You log in to apps with it, the same way everywhere, and no single company owns your login.

🔑

Access is yours to give

Every folder has a permission list. You decide who reads, who writes, who can only drop things off. Take it back and the door closes.

The questions everyone asks

Is this a cloud drive like Dropbox?

No. A drive stores files for you to open. A pod stores data that apps can understand and use with your permission. A clinic app can read just your allergy list, for 72 hours, without seeing anything else in your pod.

Is it a blockchain or crypto thing?

No tokens, no chain, no wallet. Pods use ordinary web standards (HTTP, OpenID Connect, linked data), the same plumbing the rest of the web runs on. Our audit log uses hash chaining so tampering shows, which is just good bookkeeping.

Where does my pod actually live?

Wherever you choose: on a server you run, with a provider you trust, or with us. Because Solid is an open standard, you can move your pod and every app keeps working. That is the point.

What stops an app from copying my data once it has read it?

Honestly: nothing technical, once data has left your pod. What pods change is everything before that. Apps get only what you allow, only for as long as you allow, and there is a record of who read what and when. Businesses get a reason to stop keeping copies at all: they can read it from your pod next time.

Why have pods not taken off yet?

Because most pod apps were hard to use and broke at login. The idea was right and the experience was not. That is the part we are fixing: apps that feel like apps, a guardian that handles the asking, and hosting that you can set up in an afternoon.

The rules are data, in your pod

The guardian's policy is a JSON-LD document stored in the owner's pod. First match wins, no match means "ask the owner", and nothing outside the containers you delegated can ever be granted.

// skpods/v1/consents/policy.jsonld (shortened)
{ "@type": "GuardianPolicy", "ceiling": "30d",
  "rules": [
    { "id": "never-share-ssn", "dataClasses": ["ssn"],
      "action": "deny",
      "counterOffer": { "dataClass": "insurance_member_id" } },
    { "id": "basic-health-booked-provider",
      "dataClasses": ["dob", "allergies", "medications"],
      "when": { "requesterInProviders": true, "hasBooking": true },
      "action": "approve", "maxDuration": "72h" },

      "dataClasses": ["insurance_member_id"],
      "action": "ask_owner",
      "recommend": { "action": "approve", "duration": "24h" } } ] }
⚖️
The language model never decides. Decisions come from deterministic rules you can read. An optional LLM, on any OpenAI-compatible endpoint you choose, only rewords a decision that is already made into friendlier language. If it is slow or down, nothing changes but the wording.

How the leash works

Every yes has an end date. Every end is checked.

The Solid permission system (Web Access Control) answers one question: may this identity do this, here, right now? It has no clock. So we built the part that remembers to take access back: the sk-consent broker, a small Rust service that writes permissions, removes them on time, and proves it did.

Request

A business files a request: which data, why, for how long. The person (or their delegate) sees exactly that.

Decide

The guardian applies the owner's policy: approve with a cap, ask the owner, or deny with a reason and a counter offer.

Grant

A read-only permission is written on exactly that resource, for exactly that identity. Never the whole pod, never a group.

Expire

A sweeper checks every grant each second. Grants are persisted, so a restart still revokes on time.

Verify

Revoke is checked, not assumed: the broker reads the resource as the grantee and expects a refusal. If it is not refused, it retries.

Record

Every step lands on a hash-chained audit log, mirrored into the owner's pod, so they can verify it without trusting us.

How we built it

Plain standards, all the way down

No proprietary pod API, no lock-in. Apps speak plain Solid over HTTP with DPoP-bound tokens through one small interface (PodStore). The engine underneath can change without any app noticing, and only does so after passing the same conformance gate.

An open schema for consent

Policies, requests, grants and audit entries are linked data in https://w3id.org/skpods/v1#, reusing schema.org, vCard, FOAF and Solid terms first. Any app, and any other pod provider, can read a person's consents without our software. The path forward is the W3C access request and grant work and ACP, and the model is shaped to move there.

Where this goes

Rent the compute. Own the data.

Once data lives in pods, an app stops being a place where your data is kept. It becomes compute that visits your data. That split changes what a service provider is.

Vision

A full office suite, saved to your pod

Picture a Nextcloud with real office tools (documents, spreadsheets, calendar, contacts) where your files are not on that server at all. You log in with your WebID. The server lends you CPU and RAM and the editors. Every save goes straight to your pod. Close your account and nothing is left behind, because nothing was ever kept there.

What it takes: a pod storage backend for Nextcloud's files layer and WebID login. The pod engine needs change detection (ETags) first, which the Rust engine is designed to provide. Calendars and contacts need their own mapping. The server still sees your data while it is working on it, so you choose a provider you trust for compute, and you can switch providers any time.

Vision

Your AI's memory, in your pod

Your agent's memories, preferences and history live in your pod. Any AI runtime you allow can read them, build its search index on the spot, and throw that index away when it is done. Switch providers and your agent still knows you. Revoke a provider and it forgets you, for real.
